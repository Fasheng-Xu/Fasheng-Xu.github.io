Inside a Readiness Review: 18 stages, 13 agents

Thirteen agents, one job each. Three of them test other agents' work.

A full Readiness Review is not one long prompt. It runs 18 stages: 13 AI agents, each with its own written method and only the tools its job needs, and 5 steps of plain code. What matters is what happens to each point before it reaches you.

  • One job per agent.

    Separate agents map the paper, judge fit, raise concerns, audit the proofs, the economic logic and the figures, and check the citations. None is given a tool to search or read the web.

  • Concerns are raised by one agent and tested by two others.

    A concern that fails either test is dropped, and the report's appendix keeps it with the reason. Other findings stand on their own evidence, and the report says when a finding was not independently checked.

  • Evidence first, then a cautious verdict.

    Every finding names its place in your paper and its evidence, such as a quote or a computation. A second agent re-derives the verdict without the first one's reasoning; if they differ, the more cautious one stands.

One measured run

After a revise-and-resubmit: an optional pilot

Two opposing plans for each major concern.

When a journal invites a revision, the question becomes how far to go on each concern. We run this workflow as a pilot today, following the founder's own method for planning a revision. It is separate from the Readiness Review engine, which has no revise-and-resubmit mode.

You can describe each concern in your own words, so the reports themselves need not be shared. Follow your journal's rules on AI use.

Ask about a revision pilot

  1. 01 Sort

    You describe each concern, and each is marked major or minor.

  2. 02 Argue both sides

    Each major concern goes to two agents at the same time.

    Defender Keeps what the paper contributes and looks for the smallest change that fully answers the concern.

    Reformer Asks whether the concern points to a deeper weakness, and sketches the larger change that would remove it.

  3. 03 Weigh

    The two plans are set against each other and reconciled into one recommended response. Minor concerns get a single, direct pass.

  4. 04 You decide

    You receive a memo, concern by concern, with suggested edits. Nothing is applied to your paper, and every choice stays yours.

Redesign for a classroom with AI.

Decide what students should practice on their own and where AI belongs. Our redesign method builds on the two-lane approach from published assessment guidance: a few secured assessments show what each student can do alone, and the rest are open to AI and designed for learning. Activities, assessments and rubrics then ask students to explain and defend their reasoning. What AI can do changes as new models arrive, so the plan is dated and lists what to re-test before the course runs again.

Make the next class work.

Develop lectures, slides, worked examples, assignments and rubrics around a clear learning goal. Agentic workflows draft; you review, test and decide what students see.

Give the course a through line.

Shape scattered notes into a coherent course package, with a clear sequence, consistent terminology and an instructor guide.

Let students make the decision.

Create a custom classroom simulation with roles, decision rounds and instructor controls. Use the debrief to connect what happened in the room to the ideas in the course.

Teaching pilots work from your course materials only: no student work, grades or rosters. We do not use AI-detector scores, and we do not promise that an assignment cannot be done with AI or that learning will improve.

03 / Workflows

Learn the workflow.
Keep the judgment.

Practical lessons and hands-on guidance with the skills and agentic workflows we develop, applied to your own research or teaching. We help you choose the tools, give them useful context and check what comes back.

  1. Start with your goal

    Define the question, audience and constraints. Decide what a useful result would look like.

  2. Build the context

    Choose relevant materials, methods and preferences. Inspect and correct the context before reusing it.

  3. Work in checkable steps

    Give agents defined tasks. Examine evidence, test outputs and adjust the workflow as you learn.

  4. Make the decisions

    You own the questions, research taste and learning goals. Verify the work and choose what to use.

How this differs from what you already have

You may already have a campus AI license, an AI review tool, an editor you trust or a teaching center. Each is useful. Here is what Co-Professor adds.

  1. A general chatbot or campus AI license.

    Good for many questions, and you may already have one. Co-Professor adds structure. For analytical papers: fact-checked profiles of journals and departments built from their published statements, citations looked up in bibliographic records, and separate passes that try to refute each concern before you see it. For courses: a staged redesign method in place of a blank prompt. Workflow guidance also helps you get more from the license you already have.

  2. AI review and verification tools.

    Several check a manuscript line by line, and some do it well. We see them as a complement. For analytical papers, Co-Professor works from your LaTeX source and figure code, reads the draft against the criteria of the journal or department you are targeting, and returns a revision plan you carry out. It also reaches beyond the paper, to proposals, courses and the way you work.

  3. An editor or consultant.

    A skilled human reader is worth having, and we do not replace one. Generic editing services rarely specialize in analytical OM, IS and OR models. Co-Professor is built by a business-school professor for business-school research and teaching and, where you want it, leaves you with workflows you can run yourself. Each pilot is scoped with you before it starts, at the prices below.

  4. A teaching center workshop or the AI features in your course platform.

    Workshops teach principles, and built-in tools draft materials. Co-Professor works through your own course with you: which assessments show what students can do alone, where AI belongs, and the activities, rubrics and AI policy that follow.
